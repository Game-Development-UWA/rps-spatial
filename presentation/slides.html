Gradient Ascent

If you can find the slope at any point, you can climb without seeing the whole mountain.

Take a step uphill

Move a little in the direction of the derivative. \(\alpha\) is how bold that step is: too small and you crawl, too large and you overshoot.

\[x \leftarrow x + \alpha\, f'(x)\]

A ridge, not always the summit

Ascent stops when the slope is flat. Calculus already told you that landmark might be a local maximum.

Many knobs at once

In higher dimensions the slope is the gradient: a vector of partial derivatives.

\[\theta \leftarrow \theta + \alpha\, \nabla J(\theta)\]

Single-state hill climb

No derivative, no map of the mountain. Keep one candidate. Tweak it. Move only if the tweak is better.

\[x \leftarrow x' \quad\text{if}\quad f(x') > f(x)\]

One sample vs many

Same single state; different patience with the neighbourhood.

First-choice

Sample one neighbour. If it is uphill, take it.

Steepest ascent

Sample several neighbours, then take the best of the batch.

\[x \leftarrow \arg\max_{x' \in N} f(x')\]

How you tweak

A uniform draw treats every offset in a box as equally likely. A Gaussian prefers small nudges and only rarely throws a long jump.

Uniform

Every offset in a box is equally likely.

Gaussian

Small nudges are common; long jumps are rare. \(\sigma\) is how wide that habit is.

Explore vs exploit

Larger steps wander farther. More samples squeeze more from the same neighbourhood.

I sacrificed everything for you to be here

A greedy climb stops at the first peak. Accepting a worse neighbour is a way to leave a ridge you would otherwise call home.

Simulated annealing

Take every improvement. Take a worse move with a probability that cools over time.

\[P(\text{accept}) = e^{\Delta f / T}\]

Iterated local search

Climb until you stall. Kick far enough to leave the basin, then climb again.
